You are running the CLIENT CONTEXT step of the Soothsayer method. Before you serve a client, understand two things: the problem they bring, and the client behind it. The stated question is rarely the whole story on either side. The problem has a situation, a history and stakes that frame every answer; the client has a real goal, biases and politics that shape what "a good answer" looks like to them, and that are the main route by which honest analysis quietly bends into advocacy.

Ask the user with clickable options where the answer is a choice (see `docs/asking.md`).

## The problem, from where they sit

Gather the client's own account of the problem and their world. This grounds the work. It is not yet the sharpened question; `/sooth-define` challenges and tightens it next. Here you are collecting the raw situation, not judging it.

- **Their world.** What does the client do, where do they sit in their market, and what shape are they in? Size, stage, recent trajectory, the few facts that frame everything else.
- **The problem as they live it.** In their words, what is going wrong, or what decision looms? What triggered it now, and what happens if nothing changes?
- **What is at stake.** The decision behind the engagement, who makes it, by when, and what it turns on. The money, the deadline, the bet.
- **What they already believe.** Their own read of the cause and the answer, and the evidence they lean on. Mark where it is fact and where it is assumption; the assumptions are what the work must test.
- **The terrain.** The market, the rivals, the customers, the rules, the numbers they watch. Sort it into what is known, what is contested, and what is guessed.
- **Scope and constraints.** What is in and out of the client's control, what is fixed (price, mandate, resources), what is off the table, and what has already been tried or ruled out.

## The client behind it

Now the client themselves. The same care applies: collect, do not flatter.

- **What do they really want from this engagement?** Not the stated question, the underlying goal. A decision cover, a number to justify a decision already made, ammunition for an internal fight, reassurance, or a genuine answer? Name it.
- **What are they hoping to hear?** If there is an answer they want, name it. This is the single most useful thing to know, because it is what the work must guard against delivering just because it is wanted.
- **Their biases.** Where are they likely to be over-optimistic, anchored, or blind? Sunk cost, pet projects, recency, the last consultant's frame?
- **Their motivations and incentives.** What gets them promoted, what gets them fired, whose budget or status is on the line? Who wins and who loses from each answer?
- **The politics.** Who else is in the room, who has to agree, who will resist, and what can and cannot be said out loud?

## Shape and guard

State, in one or two lines, **how this should shape the work and where it must be guarded**: the exact place the client's wants could bend the analysis, and the check that stops it. For example: "they want entry to look attractive, so set the sizing kill condition before we look, not after."

This is context, not a licence to flatter. Do not tailor the answer to what the client wants. Use the problem context to ground the work, and use the client context to make the honest answer land and to catch yourself if the work drifts towards the comfortable one. Carry the fact-versus-assumption split into `/sooth-define`.
